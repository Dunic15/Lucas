# The Product Manager and the AI Buffer — Executive Summary

**Authors:** Duccio Profeti, Edoardo Pinzauti · **Advisor:** Prof. Sergio Terzi ·
**Co-advisor:** Prof. Patrick Rau · Politecnico di Milano, Management Engineering,
2024–2025.

## The AI Buffer (core concept)

The AI Buffer is the thesis's unifying concept: AI-generated external
representations (summaries, analyses, recommendations) function as **persistent
buffers that support cue-based cognition**, while also introducing
**source-monitoring and reliance vulnerabilities**. Outcomes depend on designable
parameters of the representation (timing, structure, provenance) and on the
organizational routines that govern its use.

## The five cognitive mechanisms

1. **Encoding scaffolding** — AI summaries act as advance organizers that guide attention during later reading.
2. **Cue-dependent retrieval** — persistent AI artifacts provide retrieval cues that support recognition but may not strengthen generative recall.
3. **Source monitoring** — users must separate AI-generated from original content; vulnerable to representational fragmentation.
4. **Confidence calibration** — alignment between subjective confidence and actual accuracy under AI assistance.
5. **Cognitive load redistribution** — AI shifts attentional resources rather than simply adding information.

## Study 1 — controlled reading/memory experiment (N = 36)

- **Recognition improves with AI** (MCQ: AI M=0.598 vs No-AI M=0.510; p=.008; ~8.8 pp gain, d≈0.98).
- **Pre-reading timing is best** (accuracy 0.833 pre vs 0.568 synchronous vs 0.641 post; timing effect p<.001).
- **Free recall is unaffected** — a recognition–recall dissociation: AI strengthens cue-supported retrieval, not internally generated memory.
- **Segmented structure increases false-lure endorsement** (OR=5.93) — fragmentation weakens provenance cues.
- **Confidence calibration** is imperfect but **not worsened** by AI (no inflated overconfidence).

## Study 2 — cross-national PM survey (N = 74; 37 USA, 37 China)

- Broad AI adoption with **moderately positive** productivity (M=3.47) and decision-making (M=3.45) impact.
- **Workload reduction concentrates on routine tasks** (admin M=3.88, feedback analysis 3.84, documentation 3.70).
- **Role reconfiguration toward monitoring**: 85.1% reallocated saved time to product innovation; 75.7% said human-in-the-loop checks became more complex.
- **Governance gaps persist**: 33.8% report no clear ownership of AI-related decisions; 36.5% report no clear guidelines.

## Cross-study integration (three claims)

1. **AI-first framing works when it shapes encoding, not when it replaces it** — early exposure scaffolds encoding without adding time.
2. **Fragmentation increases misattribution risk** — provenance and structure of AI outputs are part of decision governance.
3. **Reliance is a shift in cognitive infrastructure, not mere overconfidence** — it changes who curates the representation, when it's consulted, and how it's audited.

## Six actionable takeaways

1. Use AI **early for framing**, not late for justification.
2. Prefer **integrated** representations over fragmented ones (fragmentation is an epistemic risk factor).
3. Treat AI artifacts as **part of the decision record**.
4. **Separate drafting from validation** — make verification steps explicit.
5. **Assign clear ownership** for checking AI-mediated evidence.
6. Measure success **beyond speed** — evaluate decision-trace quality (auditability, provenance, rework).

## Conclusion

AI assistance improves performance when it provides useful retrieval cues and
early framing, but introduces scalable epistemic and governance risks when
provenance is weak or verification responsibilities are unclear. **Provenance and
governance should be first-class design requirements for AI-assisted work.**
