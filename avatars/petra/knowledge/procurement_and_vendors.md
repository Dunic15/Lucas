# Procurement & Vendor Management

## Buy, build, or borrow

The make-or-buy decision weighs total cost of ownership (license + 
integration + operations + exit), strategic control (never outsource the
thing that differentiates you), speed, and risk transfer. A vendor is a
risk-transfer instrument as much as a capability: you're paying someone
else to hold delivery risk you'd otherwise hold — which only works if the
contract actually transfers it.

## Contract types and who holds the risk

- **Fixed price**: seller holds cost risk — right when scope is genuinely
  stable and specifiable; expensive change orders are the tax when it
  isn't. Watch for lowball-then-change-order as a business model.
- **Time & materials**: buyer holds cost risk — right for evolving scope;
  needs active management (caps, reviews, exit ramps) or it drifts.
- **Cost-plus** (cost + fee): buyer holds most risk; used where scope is
  unknowable — always with incentives or caps.
Hybrids (T&M with cap, fixed-price per sprint) often fit software best.
The rule: whoever can best CONTROL a risk should HOLD it, and the price
should reflect who does.

## The paper that matters

The **statement of work (SOW)** — deliverables, acceptance criteria,
timeline, dependencies on the buyer (your unfulfilled obligations are
their favorite excuse), rates, and change process. **Acceptance criteria
in the SOW** are your quality gate: vague criteria mean you own every
dispute. **Exit clauses**: termination for convenience, data return,
handover cooperation — plan the divorce while everyone's friendly. SLAs
need remedies, not just targets; an SLA without credits is decoration.

## Selecting vendors without theater

Score against weighted criteria decided BEFORE seeing bids (capability,
references on similar work, team actually assigned — not the sales team —
price, viability). Reference-check the failure question: "tell me about a
time it went wrong with them." A pilot or paid discovery beats a hundred
slides. Cheapest bid ≠ lowest cost: evaluate switching costs and the
price of their average delay against your critical path.

## Managing the relationship

A vendor on the critical path is managed like a team member with a
contract: their milestones on your schedule, their risks on your register,
a named owner on both sides, and a regular cadence with the same honesty
rules as internal status (a vendor punished for early ambers gives you
late reds). Document decisions and scope changes in writing as they
happen — the contract file you build calmly is the dispute you never have.
Escalate through the relationship ladder (working level → account → 
executive) with facts, not adjectives.
