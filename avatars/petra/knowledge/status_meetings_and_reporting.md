# Status Meetings & Reporting

## What a status meeting is for

Decisions and blockers — not narration. Facts that can be read (what shipped,
what's on track) belong in a written update *before* the meeting; the meeting
spends its minutes only on what needs humans together: decisions, trade-offs,
blockers, and risks that changed. A status meeting where nobody decided
anything and nothing was unblocked should have been an email.

## A status update that people trust

Four lines, same shape every time:
1. **Status vs plan**: on track / at risk / off track — with the date it's
   measured against.
2. **What changed since last time** (shipped, decided, learned).
3. **Top risks/blockers**, each with an owner and the help needed.
4. **The ask**: the one decision or resource this update needs from readers.

## RAG status, used honestly

Green = on plan. Amber = recoverable deviation WITH a recovery plan attached.
Red = needs intervention from outside the team. The classic dysfunction is
watermelon reporting — green outside, red inside — which usually means amber
was punished before. The PM's job is making amber safe to say early: an amber
raised in week 2 is a gift; a red discovered in week 11 is a crisis.

## Blockers: the 24-hour rule

Every blocker named in a meeting leaves with an owner (a person, not a team)
and a next checkpoint within 24–48 hours. "We're blocked on X" without an
owner is a complaint, not a blocker report. Track blocker age; anything open
longer than the agreed threshold auto-escalates — by rule, not by courage.

## Action items that actually happen

An action item has three parts or it doesn't exist: **what** (a verb and a
deliverable), **who** (one name), **when** (a date). "Someone should look
into the flaky deploys" is a wish. "Dana investigates the flaky deploys and
posts findings by Thursday" is an action. Review last meeting's actions at
the top of every meeting — the fastest culture-builder there is.

## Reporting up (steering / sponsor updates)

Sponsors want: are we on plan, what changed, what do you need from me, and is
the goal still worth it. Lead with the headline ("on track for Oct 3" / "at
risk: two-week slip likely"), keep it under a page, and never let a sponsor
learn a bad number from someone else first. Bad news travels fine when it
arrives early with a plan attached; it destroys trust when it arrives late
and bare.
