# Team Dynamics & Leadership for PMs

## Teams have a lifecycle

Tuckman's stages — forming, storming, norming, performing (and adjourning)
— are a useful map, not a law. The PM's job differs per stage: forming
needs clarity (goals, roles, working agreements — decide HOW you'll decide
before the first hard decision); storming needs conflict made safe and
explicit rather than suppressed (suppressed storming resurfaces as passive
resistance in month three); norming needs the team's own standards
reinforced; performing needs the PM to get out of the way and guard the
team's focus from the outside world. A reorg, a key departure, or a new
lead resets the clock — expect re-storming and don't panic about it.

## Motivation is not one-size

The durable finding across the research: autonomy, mastery, and purpose
beat carrots and sticks for knowledge work. Practically: connect tasks to
the why (purpose), give owners real decision room inside their scope
(autonomy), and make growth visible (mastery). Recognition works when it
is specific, timely, and public-appropriate — "thanks for catching the
migration risk before Friday" beats employee-of-the-month. Deadlines
motivate when they're believed; a schedule everyone knows is fake
demotivates precisely because it punishes the honest.

## Conflict is a tool with five settings

The classic modes: competing (assert, quick, costly to relationships),
collaborating (best answer, expensive in time), compromising (fast,
half-satisfies everyone), avoiding (right for trivia, corrosive for real
issues), accommodating (spends your position to preserve the relationship).
Skilled PMs choose deliberately — collaborate on the architecture decision,
compromise on the meeting time, never avoid the ownership dispute. Task
conflict (about the work) is healthy and should be hosted; relationship
conflict (about people) is corrosive and should be interrupted early and
privately.

## Delegation and situational leadership

Match style to the person-task pair, not to your default: high direction
for low-experience/high-stakes, high support for capable-but-uncertain,
true delegation for proven owners. Delegate OUTCOMES with constraints
("book the venue, budget X, by Friday"), not methods. The failure modes:
delegating accountability without authority (the person owns the result
but can't make the calls), and reverse delegation (their problem quietly
becomes your task list — coach, don't absorb).

## The PM's authority is mostly borrowed

In matrix organizations the PM rarely outranks anyone. Influence runs on:
credibility (say what you'll do, do it), reciprocity, the sponsor's
explicit backing (get it in the charter), and making the right thing the
easy thing (good process beats exhortation). Escalation is a legitimate
tool, spent like currency — pre-agree the triggers so using it is
procedure, not betrayal. And the unglamorous core of team leadership:
protect focus (batch interruptions, kill zombie meetings), absorb
ambiguity from above rather than radiating it downward, and give credit
away while keeping blame.
